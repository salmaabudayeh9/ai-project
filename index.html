AI is useful. It still needs someone checking it.

I use AI in my daily life, not only for studying, so I wanted to understand it better: what it actually is, where it helps, and where it has gone badly wrong.

On this page, Fact means it comes from a linked source, My view is my own opinion, and Prediction is a guess about the future.

What is AI?

Fact The OECD describes an AI system as a machine that uses the information it receives to work out an output, like a prediction, some content, a recommendation or a decision (OECD.AI).

In simple terms, AI learns from examples. Nobody writes every rule by hand. A chatbot has seen so much text that it gets very good at guessing which word should come next.

My view That is why I think "guessing" is the important word. AI doesn't know things the way we do, so it can sound completely sure of itself and still be wrong.

Where it helps

  • In daily life, it can plan a trip, explain something confusing or help write a message.
  • For studying, it can explain a topic in simpler words or make practice questions before an exam.
  • At work, it can write a first draft, summarise a long document or suggest code.
  • Image generators can turn a short description into a picture in seconds (The Washington Post).

Fact The same article points out the problems. These tools learn from the internet, so they pick up its biases, and some of the images they learn from were used without the artists' permission.

My view For me it's a good starting point, but I wouldn't let it have the final word on anything important.

Three real cases

Fraud

Everyone on the call was fake

Fact In January 2024, an employee at the engineering firm Arup in Hong Kong joined a video call with what looked like the company's finance director and other colleagues. All of them were deepfakes, AI-made copies of real people. He made 15 transfers, about US$25 million in total, to the scammers (South China Morning Post).

My view What scares me is that he did what most of us would do. For anything involving money, the safe move now is to check another way, like calling the person back.

Outage

An AI tool left to act on its own

Fact According to the Financial Times, Amazon's cloud service had at least two outages in December 2025 involving its own AI tools. One lasted around 13 hours. Amazon disagreed and called it "user error", meaning access settings had been set up wrong (Reuters, GeekWire).

My view I don't think it matters much whose fault it was. In both versions, a tool had more freedom than it should have, and nobody caught it in time.

Electricity

AI runs on real power

Fact In January 2026, PJM, which runs the largest power grid in the US, announced a plan to handle the fast rise in electricity demand from AI data centers. It came hours after the White House urged emergency action to prevent possible blackouts. PJM's chair said the goal was "keeping the lights on" while "recognizing the impact on consumers" (Reuters).

My view I had never thought of AI as something physical. But every answer comes from a building full of computers that needs power, and normal households might end up paying part of the cost.

What could come next

  • Prediction More AI "agents", tools that do tasks by themselves, will be used at work. Mistakes could spread faster if no one is watching.
  • Prediction Deepfake scams will get easier to pull off. Things like a family code word might become normal.
  • Prediction Electricity, more than the technology itself, could end up being what slows AI down.

Fact There are already guides for managing these risks. The US standards agency NIST published one that puts human oversight at the center (NIST).

My view In the end, I think how AI turns out depends less on how clever it gets and more on whether people keep checking it.

How this website was made

I wrote the content first and the code second. I picked one idea, that AI needs people checking it, and three cases that show it in different ways: fraud, an outage and electricity.

I kept the design simple on purpose: one column, an earthy green background and one serif font, so the text is easy to read. The page uses semantic HTML (header, nav, main, section, article) and one CSS file with color variables, so I can change the whole palette in one place. The diagram is an SVG with a text description for screen readers, and keyboard users can skip straight to the content.

After testing, I made the background a lighter, earthier green, made the small labels bigger so they are easier to read, and cut text to stay under 1,000 words.

AI use. I used Claude (claude.ai) to find and check sources, suggest a structure, and draft a first version of the text and code. I rewrote the text in my own words, checked every fact against the linked sources, and tested the site.
